\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent We are pleased to submit the manuscript \textbf{``SCORE-Net: Inherently
Interpretable Tabular Classification via Localized Convex Slabs''} for
consideration as a regular article in \emph{Neurocomputing}.

\noindent\textbf{What the paper contributes.} The manuscript proposes a neural
network architecture whose hidden units are, by construction, human-readable
interval rules. Each unit applies a localized triangular activation to a
bias-free linear projection, so its active region is a convex slab bounded by
two parallel hyperplanes rather than an unbounded half-space. This gives a
learning system whose internal state is inspectable at the level of individual
rules, which is the contribution we believe fits the scope of the journal: the
paper is about a neural architecture and its learning dynamics, not about
applying an existing model to a new dataset.

Three results support the claim. First, an \emph{exact bias-shifting property for pruned rules}
(Proposition 1) shows that a rule whose input weights are pruned to zero
can be absorbed into the output bias with zero change in the network's
predictions; the empirical maximum logit drift is at floating-point level
($8.94\times10^{-8}$ to $5.72\times10^{-6}$), whereas removing the same rules
without the correction costs up to 9.01 accuracy points. Second, dual $\ell_1$
regularization combined with iterative magnitude pruning reduces the models to
5--46 rules on the three binary benchmarks, with 1.2--2.7 features per rule and
96.0--98.7\% connection sparsity. Third, a rule-adaptive integer quantization
converts projection weights into small integers while the output layer stays
continuous, so compact rule sets can be scored by hand with 95.45--100\%
prediction agreement.

\noindent\textbf{Evaluation.} The model is compared against 13 baselines on four
benchmarks (COMPAS, German Credit, UCI Adult, and a 20-class synthetic task)
under a protocol in which early stopping and checkpoint selection use only the
validation partition. Two post-hoc operating points are test-informed and are
declared explicitly in the manuscript.
We report stratified-bootstrap confidence intervals, paired McNemar tests, and a
repeated stratified $5\times5$ cross-validation for every benchmark. The
paper states plainly where the model is competitive (COMPAS Global Best and
German Credit, where the difference is statistically indistinguishable from
noise), where it loses on the single split (1.26--1.57 points behind gradient
boosting on UCI Adult),
and why the synthetic advantage should not be generalized. The German Credit
comparison is reported on the shared cross-validation folds as well, where the
gap to CatBoost is 2.8--3.1 points rather than the parity seen on the single split;
we state this explicitly rather than reporting only the favorable split. Ablations isolate the
contribution of each loss term and of the initial capacity, and a separate
experiment validates the bias-shifting proposition against naive rule removal.

\noindent\textbf{Reproducibility and declarations.} All hyperparameters are fixed
per dataset and registered in the released code; the scripts that regenerate
every table and figure accompany the submission. The manuscript is not under
consideration elsewhere, has not been published previously, and both authors
approve its submission. The authors declare no competing interests. All real
datasets used are public and cited, and no new human-subject data were
collected; the synthetic benchmark is fully reproducible from the released
generator. The use of an AI-assisted writing tool for language editing is
declared in the manuscript, as required by Elsevier policy.
